title: Teaching notes

The sensei teaches from your notes, not from what it happens to know. Learners don't see the
notes themselves, only the teaching.

## DOJO.md – the whole course

Who the learners are and what holds in every lesson:

```md
- Learners know functions, arrays and loops – not how TypeScript represents missing data.
- A passing test alone doesn't show understanding: ask them to explain their code.
```

## SENSEI.md – one kata

Next to the kata's `solution.ts`. A useful one has:

- **A briefing** – the goal and the tasks. The sensei presents it when the kata starts.
- **Prerequisites** – what the learner needs, and what this kata teaches outright.
- **Where to start** – one opening question or prediction, not a lecture.
- **When help is needed** – the usual misunderstandings, each with what to explain or show and
  what to leave to the learner.
- **Review** – what to discuss once the tests pass.

```md
# Highest reading

## When help is needed

- **Missing data:** explain that `undefined` means no value. Ask how zero rainfall differs from a
  missing reading, then return to the empty-input case.
- **Negative readings:** show that -3 is warmer than -8, then ask which is warmer: -6 or -1.

Examples teach one step at a time. Never demonstrate the complete solution under another name.
```

## SENSEI.mdx – fragments to show

Name it `SENSEI.mdx` instead to give the sensei passages it may show the learner as written –
a definition, a worked example. Wrap each in `<Present id="…">`:

```mdx
<Present id="whitespace-class">
`\s` matches any whitespace character: a space, a tab or a line break.
</Present>
```

Everything outside `<Present>` stays private to the sensei. In the lesson UI it shows a fragment
by its `id`, so your notes can say when: "If they don't know the syntax, present
`whitespace-class` before explaining."

When a kata has both, `SENSEI.mdx` wins.
